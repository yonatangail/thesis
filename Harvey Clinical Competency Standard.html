The short version

Since 2020 an Italian medical degree is also the license to practice. Pavia’s Harvey Course had never written down which technical skills a graduate must have, or how independently they should perform them.

This study set that standard for 50 skills, asked faculty and sixth-year students to rate the same skills on one 0–5 scale, and compared both with the international level.

Students sit about one step below faculty expectation and nearly two below the international level. The gap is measurable, and the study proposes how to start closing it.

Background

Why a standard is needed

The Italian medical degree has been abilitante since 2020: passing it is the license to practice.

Before the reform, graduates completed a three-month practical rotation and passed a licensing examination. Today a graduate can practice independently immediately after graduation. Other systems keep a safeguard: in the United Kingdom graduates complete a supervised foundation year, and in Switzerland a national licensing examination is followed by several years of supervised specialty training.

That makes the standard set at graduation carry real weight. The Harvey Course, founded in 2009 as the first fully English-taught medical degree in Italy, follows international educational standards. Yet it had not defined a technical-skills target: which skills a graduate must master, and with what degree of independence. About 110 students are in their sixth year, and 143 places are open for the 2026/27 first-year cohort.

Two ideas the study rests on

Miller’s pyramid (1990)

Four levels: knows, knows how, shows how, and does. Traditional examinations mostly test the lower two. They show what a student knows and whether the student can describe how a skill is done. They cannot show what a student is actually able to do.

Entrustable Professional Activity (ten Cate, 2005)

An EPA turns “shows how” and “does” into a decision that can be graded: how much supervision does this person need, from observation only to fully autonomous practice? It works as an assessment tool and as a checklist of competence.

The research question

In three parts. The standard: which technical skills should a Harvey graduate master, and at what level of independence? The gap: how far below that standard do sixth-year students currently fall? The response: how can the gap begin to be closed?

“Skill” here means technical skill: the hands-on, psychomotor part of practice (physical examination, practical procedures, and treatment). Communication and other non-technical skills are outside the scope.

Methods

Four phases, from the world to Pavia

1
International reference. Review of three frameworks published at national level or above: the UK GMC (23 practical procedures with a minimum level for a new doctor), Switzerland’s PROFILES (9 core EPAs, one common level: distant, on-demand supervision) and the European Tuning project (lists skills but gives no levels).
2
Faculty survey. Faculty rated the level of supervision they expect of a new graduate for each skill. Survey in Italian.
3
Student self-assessment. Sixth-year students rated their own current level on the same skills. Survey in English.
4
Gap analysis and proposal. Comparing the three scores, finding the largest gaps, and deriving guiding principles and intervention types.

The reference level

Levels from GMC and PROFILES were converted to the 0–5 scale. The reference level of a skill is the highest converted level among the frameworks that list it. Tuning is not counted, because it has no levels. A skill listed by no framework has no reference level. The survey had 53 items in 12 categories. Three (intradermal injection, paracentesis, arthrocentesis) came from a framework later left out and have no reference level, so 50 skills are analyzed. Item wording was checked by two emergency-department attending physicians. Both surveys closed on October 1, 2026.

The scale

0
Faculty: the skill is not needed at graduation. Students: the skill is unknown to me.
1
Observe and understand.
2
Perform under direct proactive supervision: the supervisor instructs the student through each step.
3
Perform under direct reactive supervision: the supervisor intervenes or guides only when deemed necessary.
4
Perform under indirect supervision: the supervisor is available to help when asked.
5
Perform autonomously.

Adapted from the entrustment and supervision scale of ten Cate and colleagues.

Statistics

  • Both groups are summarized as means. Faculty means are rounded up from a decimal of 0.4 or above, as stated in §3.4 of the thesis. Student means are not rounded.
  • A rating of 0 means different things: for faculty it is “not required” and is left out of the faculty mean. For students it is “no exposure” and stays in the student mean. Zeros are counted separately for each skill.
  • Three gaps are calculated, and a positive value means the first term is higher: Faculty − Student, World − Student (international reference minus student mean), and World − Faculty.
  • The study is descriptive. No hypothesis is tested and no significance threshold is used, so differences are reported as point differences. Faculty responses are not split by specialty or role.
  • Free-text answers were analyzed for recurring themes. Calculations were done in Microsoft Excel and Google Sheets.

Results

What the numbers say

Who answered

Faculty

26 of 64 eligible, 40.6%

All held a professorial role: 15 professors only, 4 also head of a complex unit (Direttore di Struttura Complessa), 3 also head of a residency school, 3 holding both roles, and 1 non-tenured lecturer.

Students

45 of 110 sixth-year students, 40.9%

The survey received 144 responses across all years. Only the 45 sixth-year responses are analyzed. Other years and two Erasmus respondents are excluded.

  • Faculty expected the ceiling (mean 5, autonomous) for five skills: vital signs, comprehensive history-taking, and cardiovascular, respiratory and abdominal examination. Their lowest expectation (mean 2) was for ophthalmoscopy, spirometry, advanced airway management, ATLS and trauma care, intradermal or continuous suturing, lumbar puncture, and thoracentesis or chest tube placement.
  • Students rated themselves highest for vital signs (4.1) and abdominal examination (3.5), and lowest for thoracentesis and chest tube placement (1.1), advanced airway management and lumbar puncture (1.2 each), ophthalmoscopy and nasogastric tube (1.3 each). Zero ratings (“unknown to me”) were most frequent for ATLS and trauma care, ophthalmoscopy, and advanced airway management.
  • The largest single gaps to the international level, both 2.78 points, are advanced airway management and lumbar puncture, followed by ophthalmoscopy, nasogastric tube and ATLS (each above 2.6). Students come closest on vital signs (their mean of 4.09 slightly exceeds the reference) and are within 0.7 points on abdominal examination, 12-lead ECG and blood test interpretation.
  • Faculty expectation is itself below the international level on 27 of the 49 referenced skills, most clearly for invasive procedures (2.0 points) and airway management (1.33). The pattern reverses for five core examination skills, where faculty expect one full point more than the reference.

How much students differ from each other

Each of the 45 students was given an overall readiness score, the average of their ratings across the 50 skills. This supports the idea that without a shared target, what a student has practiced depends on rotation, supervisor and hospital.

The spread is narrowest in history-taking and physical examination (CV 38.6%) and interpretation of data and imaging (37.2%), and widest in airway management (68.8%) and resuscitation (66.1%). Routine skills give a more consistent picture than rare, specialized ones. These are self-ratings of confidence, which may reflect differences in exposure, supervised practice, or how confidently students rate themselves.

In students’ words

Ten of the 45 students answered the question on missing competencies and fifteen answered the closing comment. Five themes recurred.

Variability by rotation

Competence depends on which tirocinio, supervisor or hospital a student was assigned to.

“There may be huge gaps between some things.”

Little structured practice

The practical component is limited or inconsistent, compared with theory.

“Most procedures are only explained in class without any sort of practical counterpart.”

Skills named as missing

Most often basic life support (BLS-D), suturing, and point-of-care testing (blood tests and ABG). One student suggested formal ultrasound training, another a mandatory BLS-D course.

Lack of trust on the wards

A barrier to practice, not only a teaching gap. One student, asked whether they had done a procedure before, was shown it again after answering no, instead of being allowed to try.

Readiness for independent practice

Several linked this to the stakes of the abilitante degree.

“I really don’t feel like I can be a doctor in few months.”

Proposal

Closing the gap with what Pavia already has

The data show two kinds of gap, and they call for different responses.

A training gap (faculty − student) is where students fall below what the course itself expects. Injection techniques and catheterization lead here, and they are cheap to address. A readiness gap (world − student) is where students fall furthest below international practice: invasive procedures and airway management. Closing it will likely need resources beyond those established in this thesis.

Four guiding principles

1. Affordable, repeatable, scalable

It must work for the whole cohort every year without new funding, staff or facilities.

Example: injections and catheterization use the existing Clinical Skills Lab and 3D-printed trainers.

2. Timing

Teach when the underlying knowledge is in place and shortly before the skill is used.

Example: move wound management and suturing from the first-year elective to years 4–5, when students are on clinical rotations.

3. Context

Teach each skill inside the course that gives it meaning, a real step from “knows” to “shows how”.

Example: lumbar puncture alongside the anatomy and neurology teaching.

4. Accountability

Mandatory, logged sessions on a fixed schedule, so competence does not depend on which rotation a student draws.

Example: a fixed number of logged practice sessions for the high-gap, low-cost skills.

Three types of intervention

End-of-course workshop

A short, mandatory practical session closing each course, on its own core skills. Best fit: injections and catheterization.

Rotation-preparation course

A short workshop before the rotations of years 3 and 4, on the skills those rotations need. Best fit: invasive procedures and airway management.

Entrustment checklist

A short checklist filled in at the start of a rotation, recording the student’s level on the same 0–5 scale. It needs no new staff and addresses the lack of trust students described. Non-mandatory at first.

Not covered: the three excluded skills, thoracentesis and chest tube placement (no reference level), and communication, clinical reasoning and attitude. The proposal adds no resources beyond the Clinical Skills Lab, Lab3D4Med and existing tutoring and peer structures. Priority now: injections and catheterization. Next: invasive procedures and airway.

Limits and next steps

What this study can and cannot say

Limits

  • Both scores are self-reported, so every gap is a gap in perceived competence. Self-rated competence correlates poorly with measured performance, and negatively in at least one comparable study.
  • One survey round, not a Delphi process, and about 40% response in both groups. Both are convenience samples.
  • The reference is the higher of GMC and PROFILES, an upper bound, so international gaps read as a ceiling.
  • Three skills were excluded and one has no reference level.
  • Harvey course only, not Golgi. No subgroup comparison.
  • The intervention types have not been piloted as a complete, coordinated program.

Strengths

  • The first attempt to define a technical-skill standard for a Harvey graduate.
  • Benchmarked against three international frameworks rather than one.
  • A design comparable to a published precedent in a comparable system.
  • Separates a local training gap from a gap against international practice.

Next steps

  • A broader goal-setting exercise for year-by-year targets, with the Ordine dei Medici di Pavia, clinicians at San Matteo and recent graduates.
  • Wider data: closer to a census, a student survey in February, and an extension to Golgi.
  • Pilot the interventions, and use a full Delphi process.
  • Validate self-ratings against observed performance (OSCE or workplace assessment).

Conclusion

The gap is measurable. Closing it is the next step.

The study defined, for the first time, a technical skill standard for a graduating Harvey student across 50 skills, benchmarked against international frameworks and set against faculty expectations. Sixth-year students’ self-perceived competence falls short of the faculty standard and, by a wider margin, of the international reference, most sharply for invasive procedures, catheterization and airway management.

It proposes four principles and three types of intervention, starting with the skills where an affordable response is possible now, and recommends a broader goal-setting exercise for the categories where the shortfall is largest.
